Risk Factors

What Raises Your Risk?

A risk factor is anything that raises your chance of getting a disease. Some risk factors you can change. Others you can’t. Knowing them helps you make a plan.

Risk factor 1

Knowledge gaps

HPV can spread without any signs or symptoms (changes you can see or feel), so people may not know they have it (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2024a). Many students also don’t know that HPV can cause cancer or that a vaccine can prevent it.

What You Can Do

Learn the basics on the Home page, then share what you learned with a friend.

Risk factor 2

Attitudes and beliefs

Some people fear vaccine side effects (unwanted reactions to a vaccine or medicine). Others feel embarrassed about screening (tests that look for problems early, before you feel sick).

What You Can Do

Many people have no side effects from the HPV vaccine, and the most common ones are mild, like a sore arm, headache, or feeling tired (CDC, 2024b).

Risk factor 3

Social environment

Friends, partners, family, and social media misinformation (false information) can discourage vaccination.

What You Can Do

Check what you hear against trusted health sources. Start with the Myth vs. Fact box below.

Social environment

Myth vs. Fact

Words to know: a Pap test checks the cervix (the lower part of the uterus, or womb) for cell changes. Abnormal means not normal.

Myth: “If I’m over 18, it’s too late.”

Fact: HPV vaccination is recommended for everyone through age 26 who is not already vaccinated (CDC, 2024c).

Myth: “I’d know if I had HPV.”

Fact: HPV can spread even when there are no signs or symptoms (CDC, 2024a).

Myth: “An abnormal Pap test means I have cancer.”

Fact: An abnormal result usually does not mean cancer, and in most cases treatment prevents cervical cancer from developing (CDC, 2025b).

Myth: “I got the vaccine, so I don’t need screening.”

Fact: Vaccinated women should still get regular cervical cancer screening starting at age 21 (CDC, 2025a).

Risk factor 4

Sexual behavior

People who become sexually active at a young age, especially before 18, or who have multiple sexual partners are more likely to be infected with a high-risk type of HPV, meaning a type that can lead to cancer (National Cancer Institute [NCI], 2024).

What You Can Do

Talk openly with partners, and use the steps on the Prevention page, like condoms and the HPV vaccine.

Risk factor 5

Access and cost

Transportation, clinic hours, cost, and not knowing where to go can keep students from getting care. Most health insurance plans cover vaccines (CDC, 2024b), and in Ohio most women have insurance that covers cervical cancer screening at no cost (Ohio Department of Health, n.d.).

What You Can Do

See the Local Resources page for low-cost and no-cost options in Cincinnati and Ohio.

Risk factor 6

Biological and health factors

  • A high-risk HPV infection that lasts for years is the main cause of cervical cancer (NCI, 2024).
  • A weakened immune system (the body’s defense against germs) lowers the body’s ability to fight HPV (NCI, 2024). Women living with HIV (a virus that weakens the immune system) are 6 times more likely to develop cervical cancer than women without HIV (World Health Organization [WHO], 2026).
  • Smoking or breathing in secondhand smoke (smoke from someone else’s cigarette) increases the risk of cervical cancer (NCI, 2024).

What You Can Do

You can’t control every risk, but you can choose not to smoke. Tell your health care provider about any health conditions you have.

Quick Takeaway

Some risk factors, like a long-lasting high-risk HPV infection or a weakened immune system, are hard to control (NCI, 2024). Many others, like smoking, knowing the facts, and getting care, are things you can act on today.
