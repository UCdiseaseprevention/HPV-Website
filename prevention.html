Prevention and Control

How to Protect Yourself

Prevention means stopping a disease before it starts, or finding it early when it is easier to treat. These seven steps work best together. Start with the checklist, then read the details for each step.

Step 1

Get the HPV vaccine

A vaccine is a shot that teaches your body to fight a germ. The HPV vaccine is recommended for everyone through age 26 who is not already vaccinated (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2024c).

Started before age 15

2 doses

If started before age 15, 2 doses are needed, given 6 to 12 months apart (CDC, 2024b).

Started at age 15 or older (includes anyone starting in college)

3 doses

If started at age 15 or older, 3 doses are given over 6 months (CDC, 2024b).

How well it works

Over 90%

HPV vaccination can prevent over 90% of cancers caused by HPV (CDC, 2025a).

A dose is one shot. Adults 27–45 can talk with a doctor about whether it’s right for them (CDC, 2024b).

Step 2

Get screened on schedule

Screening means testing for a problem early, before you have symptoms. It is for anyone with a cervix (CDC, 2025b).

Ages 21–29

Pap test every 3 years if results are normal (CDC, 2025b).

Ages 30–65

Talk to your doctor about one of three options (CDC, 2025b):

  • An HPV test alone every 5 years
  • An HPV test plus a Pap test (co-testing) every 5 years
  • A Pap test alone every 3 years

The Pap test looks for cell changes on the cervix (the lower part of the uterus, or womb) (CDC, 2025b).

The HPV test looks for the virus that can cause those changes (CDC, 2025b).

Step 3

Follow up on abnormal results

  1. Get tested
  3. Wait for results (up to three weeks)
  5. Not normal? Follow up right away

Results can take up to three weeks (CDC, 2025b). If cells are not normal, follow up with your doctor right away, because treatment in most cases prevents cervical cancer from developing (CDC, 2025b).

Step 4

Use condoms

Condoms can lower the risk of passing HPV but do not completely prevent it, because HPV can infect areas the condom doesn’t cover (National Cancer Institute [NCI], 2024).

Step 5

Limit the number of sexual partners

Having multiple partners raises the chance of getting a high-risk HPV type, which is a type that can lead to cancer (NCI, 2024).

Step 6

Don’t smoke, and avoid secondhand smoke

Don’t smoke, and avoid secondhand smoke, which is smoke from someone else’s cigarette (CDC, 2024c; NCI, 2024).

Step 7

Use free or low-cost help

Ohio’s Breast and Cervical Cancer Project offers no-cost cervical cancer screening to people who qualify (Ohio Department of Health, n.d.).

Quick Takeaway

If you’re 26 or younger and not fully vaccinated, ask about the HPV vaccine (CDC, 2024c). Starting at age 21, get screened on schedule and follow up on any abnormal result (CDC, 2025b).
